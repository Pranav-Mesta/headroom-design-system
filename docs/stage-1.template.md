## The idea everything follows from

**Headroom** is a money app for young professionals in India (25–32). The name has two meanings: room left in the budget, and room to breathe. The visual system makes the first meaning literal so the user feels the second.

**Clearance** is the core idea. Money left is drawn as *open space above a dashed limit line*, like the clearance sign on a car-park entrance. Most finance apps show "₹33,550 spent (64%)" in a progress ring, which asks the user to read a failure metric. Headroom shows "₹18,450 of room", the answer to the question users actually have: *am I okay?*

Every foundation below either serves that gauge or stays out of its way.

**How it was chosen:** four directions were sketched as Home screens: Margin Notes (stationery), Clearance (signage and sky), Boarding Pass, and the category standard of white cards with an indigo accent. Clearance won because it is the only one where the *product's name, its mechanism and its visual signature are one idea*. Five more directions were rejected outright, and each left behind one rule the system keeps:

| From | Rule kept |
|---|---|
| Type specimen | Hierarchy comes from scale contrast, not extra boxes or color. |
| Orienteering map | One reserved color with one meaning: clearance blue means "you / today / act". |
| Streaming wall | Selecting a chart series lifts it and dims the rest. |
| Botanical folio | Compared periods always share one fixed scale; charts never silently rescale. |
| Monochrome canon | Every insight sentence sits next to the number that proves it. |

### Principles (tie-breakers for every later decision)

1. **Clarity before alarm.** State plainly, then warn proportionately.
2. **No shame.** Spending is shown in neutral ink. Color never moralises.
3. **Numbers are the interface.** Amounts get the brand face, tabular figures and the most care.
4. **Redundant meaning.** Every status is encoded at least twice: color plus a sign, icon or word.
5. **One system, three surfaces.** Platform-neutral tokens that map 1:1 to Figma variables.

## 1. Color

### 1.1 How the palette was built

All ramps are generated in **OKLCH**, not picked by eye. OKLCH is perceptually uniform, so `500` has the same perceived lightness in blue, yellow and green. That makes the rule "text uses 700+, fills use 50–300" hold across every hue instead of being re-checked per color. The generator fits every color into sRGB by reducing chroma, never by shifting hue, then **measures every declared text/background pairing in both modes and refuses to build if any falls below WCAG AA**. The first build failed three pairings (secondary text on sunken wells and on the headroom fill, and input borders on sunken wells). Those were fixed by moving `text/secondary` to `neutral/700` and `border/default` to `neutral/600` in light mode. That trail is the evidence that the ratios below are measured, not claimed.

**Why these hues**

- **Blue (brand), hue 258.** Blue is the trust color of finance, which is a feature for an app holding salary data. The risk is that blue-on-white is where most fintech already lives, so distinctiveness comes from the *gauge*, not the hue. This is a deliberate trade: the color stays familiar and the form carries the identity.
- **Neutral, hue 250, chroma 0.006–0.022.** Greys and blacks carry a faint blue cast so the whole UI sits under one light source. Pure grey next to a blue brand reads dirty.
- **Yellow (near-limit), hue 70, saffron.** A warm saffron rather than a lemon or mustard yellow. It appears only when a budget passes 80%. Fills use the soft stop (`yellow/300`); words use the dark stop (`yellow/800`), because yellow text is unreadable on white.
- **Red (over-limit and destructive), hue 22.** A true red, so "over" reads instantly, but the fill uses the medium stop (`red/400`) rather than full strength. The words ("₹740 over") carry the weight in deep red; the bar stays calm. Apple, Carbon and Primer all pair a bright fill with a darker text shade in the same way.
- **Green (income), hue 150, and Teal (savings), hue 200.** Kept apart by roughly 50° so savings never reads as income. Teal is cooler and calmer: money resting, not money arriving.

**Why 0 / 950 / 1000 exist on neutral.** The brief asks for 50–900. Neutrals need two more steps: pure white for raised light surfaces, and a ground darker than 900 for dark mode. A dark UI on 900 (#22282F) looks washed out at night, and AMOLED users expect near-black.

**Every colour has a name.** Paths are for code (`color/neutral/900`); names are for people (**Neutral Light Black**). Chromatic stops follow one pattern: Faint (50), Pale (100), Light (200), Soft (300), Medium (400), True (500), Strong (600), Deep (700), Dark (800), Darkest (900). Neutrals use words people already say: White, Off-White, Mist, Light Grey, Silver, Mid Grey, Grey, Dark Grey, Graphite, Charcoal, Light Black, Black, Deep Black. A name never changes while its path exists (see [governance](governance.md)).

### 1.4 Finance tokens

{{finance}}

**The decision to defend: expenses are not red.** Most money apps color every debit red, so a normal Tuesday (coffee, metro, lunch) looks like a list of failures. Headroom sets expenses in `text/primary` with a true minus sign (U+2212 −, not a hyphen). Red-family color is spent only where the user crossed a limit *they set*. This is the "no shame" principle expressed as a single token.

**Meaning never relies on color alone (WCAG 1.4.1).** Every finance state has a redundant non-color encoding. Stage 2 components must keep all of them:

| State | Color token | Non-color encoding (required) | Screen reader |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income | `finance/income` | `+` sign, "Received" in row subtitle | "plus eighty-four thousand five hundred rupees, received" |
| Expense | `finance/expense` | `−` sign (U+2212) | "minus three hundred eighty-six rupees" |
| Near limit | `finance/near-limit` | warning-triangle icon + "Close to limit" | "Food, eighty-two percent used, close to limit" |
| Over budget | `finance/over-budget` | ▲ caret icon + "₹740 over" + fill crosses the dashed line | "Shopping, seven hundred forty rupees over budget" |
| Savings | `finance/savings` | piggy/target icon + "Saved" label | "Goa trip, thirty-eight thousand of sixty thousand saved" |
| No change | `finance/neutral-change` | `±0` or "Same as last month" | "same as last month" |

## 2. Typography

### 2.1 Typeface: Anek Latin, plus native UI faces

**Why Anek Latin.** For a ₹ app, four properties are non-negotiable. I measured them with `fontTools` against the actual font files instead of trusting specimen pages:

1. **A native ₹ glyph (U+20B9).** Without it, the OS substitutes a fallback rupee from another font, with a different weight and baseline, on every single amount.
2. **Tabular figures (`tnum`)**, so amounts align in columns and don't jitter while being typed on the keypad.
3. **A slashed or dotted zero (`zero`)**, so 0 never reads as O in reference numbers and UPI IDs.
4. **Narrow punctuation under tabular figures.** Indian grouping puts commas in almost every amount (1,00,000), so a comma that widens to figure width makes every number look broken.

Of 20 shortlisted grotesques, 9 have no ₹ glyph and 3 more lack tabular figures. 8 pass both, and only 2 also have a slashed zero.

**The fourth test came from building, not research.** My first pick was Schibsted Grotesk. When I set amounts in it on the spec site, `₹18,450` rendered as `₹18 , 450`: its tabular feature widens the comma and period from 543 to 1,300 units, the full width of a figure. Measuring the punctuation advance widths of all 8 finalists showed the same flaw in Public Sans, and showed that **Anek Latin keeps its comma at 397 units with or without `tnum`**. It is the only face that passes all four tests:

{{fontaudit}}

**Character fit.** Anek Latin was drawn by Ek Type (Mumbai) as the Latin member of a multi-script family, so its ₹ is designed as part of the font rather than bolted on, without the face looking regional. Its open, slightly squared forms read like wayfinding signage, which suits Clearance.

**The width axis does a real job.** Anek is variable in weight (100–800) *and* width (75–125). Mobile finance UIs constantly run out of horizontal room (chart labels, chips, keypad history). Instead of shrinking the size below the 12px floor, `type/amount/compact` condenses to 85% width at the same size. One file covers every weight and width.

**Why two families.** On iOS and Android, body text, labels and controls use the platform face (SF Pro / Roboto). They respond to Dynamic Type and font-scale settings with no extra work, and they feel native, as both Apple's HIG and Material recommend. The brand face is reserved for display, titles and **every amount**, so the brand lives exactly where principle 3 says it should: in the numbers. On web there is no native face worth matching, so Anek Latin does both jobs.

In Figma, `font/family/ui` is a string variable with three modes (iOS, Android, Web). One component library then renders correctly per platform by switching mode.

{{fontfamily}}

### 2.2 Type scale

The scale is a **~1.25 ratio**, snapped to whole pixels and a 4px line-height grid. That's large enough to give clear steps on a phone, small enough that `title-lg` doesn't crowd a 390-wide screen. `body-lg` is 17/24 to match iOS Body, the size users have already calibrated their reading to. **12px is the floor**: nothing ships smaller, which also keeps every size legible at 200% scaling.

{{typescale}}

Sizes are in px for Figma and web. They map to **pt (iOS)** and **sp (Android)** one-to-one, so they scale with the user's text-size setting.

### 2.3 Numeric styles

Amounts get their own styles because they behave differently from words. They always use **tabular, lining figures with a slashed zero**, so a column of ₹ values aligns on the decimal and a balance doesn't shift width while it updates.

{{numeric}}

**Apply `tnum` only to numeric styles, never to body text.** Tabular digits in running prose look loose, and in some faces (Schibsted Grotesk, Public Sans) the feature also widens punctuation, so every sentence gets gaps before its commas. The `type/amount/*` styles carry the feature; `type/body-*` and `type/label-*` don't.

**Formatting rules that travel with these styles** (Stage 3 expands them):

- **Indian grouping:** 1,00,000, not 100,000. Use `Intl.NumberFormat('en-IN')` / `NumberFormatter(locale: en_IN)`.
- **Sign:** U+2212 minus and `+` sit before the ₹: `−₹386`, `+₹84,500`.
- **Paise:** shown only where they exist (transaction detail, keypad entry). Lists round to the rupee.
- **Lakh/crore abbreviations** (`₹1.2L`, `₹3.4Cr`) only in chart axes and tight chips, never in the primary amount.
- The **₹ symbol is set at 70% of the figure size** in `amount/hero` and `amount/lg`, raised to cap height. The number carries the meaning; the currency is context.

A **4px base** with an 8px rhythm. 4px exists for the tight internal gaps that mobile finance rows need. Everything between components snaps to multiples of 8.

## 4. Radius, elevation, borders, grid

### 4.1 Radius

Signage corners are *softened*, not round: firm enough to feel trustworthy, never bubbly. Radius grows with component size so nested corners stay concentric (inner radius = outer radius − padding).

{{radius}}

### 4.2 Elevation and shadow

Light mode uses **two-layer shadows** (a tight contact shadow plus a soft ambient one), tinted with `neutral/1000` instead of black so they belong to the sky ground. Dark mode switches to **tonal elevation** (lighter surfaces) because shadows disappear on near-black. Only four levels exist; a fifth would mean the hierarchy itself is wrong.

{{elevation}}

### 4.3 Borders

{{border}}

The **clearance line** is the system's only ornament: a 2px, 6-on/4-off dashed line marking every limit (a budget, a goal target, the month). It is never used as a decorative divider; if it appears, a limit is being shown.

### 4.4 Grid and breakpoints

Breakpoints follow **Material 3 window size classes** (600 / 840 / 1200) rather than device names. Android, iPad split-view and the web dashboard then all respond to the same thresholds, and the classes describe available space, not hardware.

## 5. Iconography

**Style:** outline icons on a 24px grid, round caps and joins, and a stroke that scales with size so optical weight stays even. Every icon comes from **[Not Boring Icons](https://www.notboringicons.com)** (stroke style), re-stroked to these tokens. Four meanings aren't in their library yet and use stand-ins (see `docs/icon-inventory.md`). System chrome (back, share, close) uses **SF Symbols on iOS and Material Symbols on Android**. Users already know those positions and shapes, and redrawing them is the classic "ported from another platform" tell.

Category icons always sit in a **40px tile** (`radius/md`, a tinted surface) so they read as a set and give list rows a steady left edge. Icons are never the only label. In charts and the category picker, the category name always appears too.

{{icon}}

## 6. Motion

Motion in a money app has one job: **show cause and effect** (this tap added that amount; this fill is your month). It is never there to entertain. Durations are short because users open the app in 10-second bursts. Easing follows Material's emphasized curves, which read as calm: fast to start, long gentle settle.

{{duration}}

{{easing}}

**When to animate**

- ✅ Gauge and progress fills, **once** per first view of a period, showing how the month got here.
- ✅ Sheets, toasts and page transitions, following each platform's native model.
- ✅ Press feedback within 120ms, so every tap is acknowledged.
- ✅ Goal reached: one `deliberate` celebration, then never again for that goal.
- ❌ **Never count up balances or amounts.** A ticking number makes the user wait to learn whether they're okay, and it can't be read mid-animation.
- ❌ No looping or attention-seeking motion on warnings. An over-budget state that pulses is shouting.
- ❌ No parallax or decorative scroll effects.

**Reduced motion** (iOS Reduce Motion, Android Remove animations, `prefers-reduced-motion`): spatial movement becomes a crossfade at `duration/fast`, and fills appear at their final value.

## Decisions to defend in a portfolio review

1. **"Why aren't expenses red?"** Because most transactions *are* expenses, and coloring the normal case as an alarm creates the money anxiety the product exists to reduce. Red-family color is reserved for crossing a limit the user chose. Meaning is still never color-only: the − sign and the word "over" carry it for colour-blind users and screen readers.
2. **"Why Anek Latin and not Inter or a system font?"** Because the font was chosen by measurement, and the measurement overturned my first choice. Of 20 candidates, 9 had no ₹ glyph, and only 2 had a native ₹, tabular figures *and* a slashed zero. Building the spec site then exposed a fourth failure: Schibsted Grotesk widens tabular commas to figure width, which breaks Indian-grouped amounts. Anek Latin is the only face that passes all four tests, and its width axis lets tight amounts condense instead of shrinking. UI text still uses SF Pro / Roboto on phones, so the brand face appears only where the brand lives: in the numbers. *(Telling this story well shows process: a hypothesis, a test, and a reversal based on evidence.)*
3. **"Why show money left instead of money spent?"** The clearance gauge is a framing decision, not decoration. "₹18,450 of room" answers *am I okay?* directly, while "64% spent" makes the user do the math and reads as a running score of failure. The dashed clearance line is the one visual signature, and tokenising it (`finance/headroom`, `finance/clearance-line`, `border/dash/clearance`) means the idea survives into every component and the Figma library.
